# Performance Protocol — Body, Environment, and Test-Day Execution

The fourth research layer: not what to study, but how to make the studying stick
biologically, where/how to do the sessions, how to execute on test day under
no-partial-credit conditions, and the math-readiness patch kit. Evidence strength is
flagged honestly; full citations in `research-report.md` §10.

---

## 1. The biology rules (sleep, exercise, caffeine)

1. **Consistent 7–9h sleep for ALL 7 weeks — consistency itself is the lever.** In the MIT
   study, sleep duration/quality/consistency explained ~25% of grade variance — and sleep
   the night before a test showed NO rescue effect. You can't cram sleep. [correlational
   but strong pattern]
2. **Never an all-nighter.** One night of deprivation cuts the brain's ability to form new
   memories ~40% (experimentally proven with fMRI). The all-nighter sabotages the very
   studying it's for. [HIGH confidence]
3. **Sleep AFTER studying is part of studying** — consolidation happens in sleep. Put a
   short review of the hardest material right before bed; don't trade sleep hours for it.
4. **Short on sleep? Take a 60–90 min nap** (long enough to hit deep + REM sleep), not a
   20-minute one, when memory is the goal.
5. **20-min brisk walk before a session** primes encoding; a walk after a heavy session
   may aid consolidation. Real but modest effects. [meta-analytic, heterogeneous]
6. **Caffeine: fine to start a session, hard curfew 6+ hours before bed** (RCT: caffeine
   even 6h pre-bed cut sleep by an hour — quietly destroying rule 1).
7. **Study your hardest material at your personal peak time of day**; for most
   college-age people that's late morning through evening.
8. **Exam-stress insurance is already built into the system:** retrieval practice makes
   memories resistant to acute stress (Science RCT: self-tested material survived stress
   that erased restudied material). Every quiz session is stress-proofing.

## 2. Session blueprint (~90 min) and environment

| Time | What |
|---|---|
| 0–3 min | Phone silenced, **in another room**. Only MyLab/course tabs open. |
| 3–35 min | Block 1: problems — **on paper first**, then enter into MyLab. |
| 35–43 min | Real break: walk/stretch/water, outside if possible. **Never the phone** (phone breaks restored nothing in the lab — 22% fewer problems solved after). |
| 43–80 min | Block 2: problems + review of anything missed. |
| 80–90 min | Wind-down: update tracker, then 5–10 min of quiet nothing (wakeful rest measurably boosts retention of what you just learned). |

Environment rules, honest evidence flags:
- **Phone in another room** — "mere presence" evidence is contested, but notification
  disruption and the students-self-interrupt-within-6-minutes finding are solid, and the
  fix is free. Working memory (exactly what stats taxes) is the effect that survives the
  skeptical meta-analyses.
- **No lyrical music during problem-solving** (hurts memory/reading tasks whether you
  like the song or not). Quiet instrumental or silence during; pump-up music *before/
  between* blocks is the legitimate use. White noise only to mask household noise.
- **Break at natural stopping points** — the 25/5 Pomodoro ritual has no evidence
  advantage (the one direct test found imposed breaks slightly *worse* than
  self-regulated ones); taking real breaks does.
- **Paper first, screen second.** Writing the steps externalizes working memory, creates
  the error-diagnosis record (formula slip vs arithmetic slip vs rounding), and separates
  thinking from answer-entry. (The famous "handwriting beats laptop" notes study didn't
  replicate cleanly — this rec rests on the problem-solving logic, not that headline.)
- **Mostly study at your usual desk** (it matches your online-test context); take
  occasional review sessions elsewhere; run timed practice exams at the exact desk/setup
  where you'll take the real ones.

## 3. Math-readiness patch kit (do BEFORE July 1)

The research surprise: what sinks intro-stats students is **6th–8th-grade math**, not
algebra II. A 15-question basic-skills quiz predicted stats grades; having taken calculus
predicted nothing. The killers, in order: fractions/decimals/percents (38% of stats
students couldn't divide 1/2 by 2/3), order of operations in formulas, calculator
parentheses (12/6/36 = 0.056 but 12/(6/36) = 72 — and TI-84 says −3² = −9), solving
simple equations, square roots, negatives.

**The 15-item self-diagnostic** (paper first; redo #6–12 on the TI-84; target 13+/15):

1. Write 0.3% as a decimal. → **0.003**
2. 1% chance of winning, 1,000 players — about how many winners? → **10**
3. Larger: 4/7 or 5/8? → **5/8**
4. 1/2 ÷ 2/3 = ? → **3/4**
5. True/false: 0.049 < 0.05 → **True** (this IS the p-value comparison)
6. −3² and (−3)² → **−9 and 9**
7. 12 ÷ 6 ÷ 36 and 12 ÷ (6 ÷ 36) → **≈0.056 and 72**
8. z = (68 − 74)/4 → **−1.5**
9. Solve 1.96 = (x − 100)/15 → **x = 129.4**
10. Data {2, 4, 9}: x̄ and s = √(Σ(x−x̄)²/(n−1)) → **x̄ = 5, s = √13 ≈ 3.61**
11. √40 lies between which whole numbers? → **6 and 7**
12. SE = σ/√n, σ = 20, n = 25 → **4**
13. Calculator shows 2.3E-4 — as a decimal? → **0.00023**
14. 40 yes out of 250: proportion and percent → **0.16 and 16%**
15. "x is at least 30" in symbols; does "at least 2" include 2? → **x ≥ 30; yes**

Misses on 1–5/14 → Khan Academy fractions/decimals/percents units (~3–4 hrs, highest
yield). Misses on 6–8/12 → order-of-operations unit + retype those items on the TI-84
until calculator and hand agree. Miss on 9 → linear-equations unit (~1 hr). Item 10 alone
rehearses ~80% of the course's hand computation — drill it until smooth.

**Confidence case:** in a randomized trial, students who *failed algebra placement* were
assigned to college statistics with weekly support — 56% passed (vs 39% in remedial
algebra) and they graduated at higher rates. Statway: triple the success in half the
time. You do not need to be "a math person." The gaps are patchable in a weekend.

## 4. Test-day execution (no partial credit = precision warfare)

### 24 hours out
- **No new material.** Only retrieval practice of known material, drilled to automatic —
  retrieval-strength is what survives pressure (choking research: pressure eats working
  memory; automatic answers don't need it).
- **One timed practice set under simulated test conditions** (the relevant boss battle,
  re-fought). From it, write your per-question time budget with checkpoints.
- Full night's sleep (rule 1). Normal breakfast, water at the desk [light evidence,
  cheap]. Warm up with 3–5 easy already-mastered problems; touch nothing shaky in the
  last hour. If genuinely anxious: 10 min of writing out your worries right before
  [original RCTs strong, replications mixed — free lottery ticket].

### During the test
- **Pacing:** check the clock only at planned checkpoints (every ~5 questions). If
  backtracking is allowed: two passes — bank everything solvable fast, flag the rest. If
  backtracking is BLOCKED: hard cap ~1.5× average time per question, and ALL checking
  happens before clicking Next. Never leave a blank.
- **Mark your confidence as you go** (K = known, G = guess, on scratch paper). Real-time
  confidence is a valid signal; post-hoc gut feeling is not. The G-list is your revision
  queue.
- **Answer-changing policy — the data, not the folklore:** across 1,561 exams, answer
  changes were wrong→right 51% vs right→wrong 25% (2:1), confirmed across 33 studies.
  CHANGE when you have a concrete reason (misread the stem, found a computational error,
  recalled the fact) or when revising a flagged guess with new reasoning. Don't change on
  free-floating unease alone. "Trust your first instinct" is a documented myth.
- **The numeric-error gauntlet** (humans err 1–5% per calculation step and catch <half by
  re-reading — expect 1–3 self-generated errors per test if you never check):
  1. Estimate before computing; computed answer must agree with the estimate.
  2. **Re-derive, don't re-read** — re-key the calculation fresh, ideally by a different
     route (complement rule, alternate formula).
  3. Sanity gauntlet: probabilities & p-values in [0,1] · r in [−1,1] · SD/SE ≥ 0 ·
     SE < SD · CI contains the point estimate · higher confidence = wider · n is a whole
     number rounded UP · mean inside the data's range · |z| or |t| rarely > 5.
  4. Re-read the QUESTION before clicking Next: one- vs two-tailed? proportion or
     percent? σ or σ²? exact rounding/format demanded?

### Panic protocol (in-test)
1–3 **physiological sighs** (double nasal inhale, long slow exhale — beat meditation in a
Stanford RCT for rapid arousal reduction) → say "**I'm excited**" (reappraisal
outperformed "calm down" on math tasks) → jot the intrusive worry in one line on scratch
paper → resume. And remember: everything on the test has survived your boss battles —
the retrieval practice already stress-proofed it.

## 5. What the tutor changes (now in `tutor-protocol.md`)

Step-level feedback per the tutoring-science playbook: right+confident → terse confirm,
harder next; right+unsure → full explanation (highest-payoff moment); wrong on new
material → hint → retry → worked example → isomorphic retry; wrong+confident → immediate
direct correction + explain-why-the-wrong-answer-tempted-you + scheduled re-test (those
errors resurface after ~a week if not re-tested); help fades one notch after success,
rises one notch after failure; difficulty stays near the 85% sweet spot.
